# Story 6 — Framing & Staging Reference for the Rebuild

Sources: TED-Ed "Why can't you put metal in a microwave?" (Aaron Slepkov, dir. Yuriy Polyashko / Darvideo, 5:20, 2.1M plays) — full transcript + production descriptions; secondary frame descriptions of the same story's staging; our Ricks Clips analysis (`ricks-topic-engine/SKILL.md`, `ricks_formula_2026-11-21.md`). Written to answer the owner's six complaints about board #1.

## What the references actually do

**TED-Ed (same story, same beats):**
- The whole invention story — candy bar, popcorn, egg, first oven — is told as a **fast montage of single-action vignettes in the first ~35 seconds** of a 5:20 video. One beat = one visual = one clear action. No beat lingers; the camera never admires a set.
- Each story beat is staged **object-first**: Spencer standing by the magnetron → the bar melting → kernels popping → egg exploding **onto the colleague** (the payoff is framed on the victim's reaction, not on the egg) → the first oven appears.
- After "So, how does it work?" the film **leaves the story world entirely**: cutaway/diagram mode — electromagnetic spectrum chart, polar-molecule diagram (water molecules rotating), a **cross-section cutaway of the magnetron and oven cavity with the waves visibly bouncing inside the box**, then isolated demo vignettes (marshmallow puffing, butter melting "sometimes violently") against plain backgrounds. The mechanism is SHOWN inside a cutaway, never described over a normal shot.
- The first oven is a **scale gag**: a 6-foot, 750 lb machine presented as *taller than the people next to it*, then a shrink through time to today's countertop oven. A human body is the ruler in the same frame.
- It ends on a **graphic device** (the 3-question checklist), not on a scene.

**Ricks Clips (our house benchmark):**
- Simple white-faced characters inside **rich, dressed 3D environments** — the density lives in the SET, never in the characters. Every frame has foreground / midground / background occupied.
- Camera **always moving**: reframe or push every 1.5–2.5 s, no exceptions. Pushes go toward the story object; pull-backs are reserved for reveals and scale.
- The mocker gets the close-up; the hero stays silent. The mechanism is shown in-scene with a daily-life analogy (night-blue light cue at the "You see" beat). The payoff is **shown, lands on the mocker, and the film ends on the last word** (loop).
- Object-led stories are his strongest format (Ancient Jar 35M): **one object + one place, drawable in a single frame**. The object is the star; everything else is staging around it.

## Rules per problem beat

### S01 — the pocket (was: giant floating chocolate slab)
- The bar is a prop **attached to the body**, not a billboard: it sits IN the chest pocket at ≈ y 2.0 on the 2.9-tall hero — pocket patch visible, bar half-sunk with only its top third emerging, melted drip running **down the coat cloth** (contact = believable; floating beside him = fake).
- Size discipline: even in the opening tight frame, the bar itself is **≤ ~35% of frame width**. If the chocolate is the biggest thing in frame, the camera is wrong, not the prop.
- Owner's move, TED-Ed's logic: **open tight on the pocket, then dolly OUT** to the waist-up reveal (hero + radar cabinet behind him). A pull-back is a reveal; a push-in on a small prop just inflates it.
- Keep a foreground layer in the reveal (bench edge across the bottom of frame) so the shot has depth the moment it widens.

### S05 — the shock face (was: floating head, body eaten by the bench)
- Reaction close-up ≠ head-only. Frame **mid-torso up**: head height **≤ ~55% of frame height**, shoulders, collar and at least one arm/hand always in frame. Ricks' mocker close-ups keep the shoulders — the face plays against the body.
- Fix the staging, not just the lens: step the hero **0.8–1.0 unit toward camera** from the bench line for this beat so the bench edge crosses at hip height or drops below the frame — never let furniture cut the character at the neck.
- Camera at face height (y ≈ 2.5), straight on. The expression (S05's ring eyes + O mouth already work) only reads as "shock" when the body under it recoils — keep the torso in so the lean-back can play.

### S09 — popcorn (was: invisible)
- Stage it as an **eruption in the central column**: bowl of kernels in the foreground at bench height, dead center; popped corn must fly **up past head height and rain back down** — if the popcorn never leaves the bowl's silhouette, the beat doesn't exist.
- Contrast rule: bright action against the **darkest background element** (the cabinet). White popcorn in front of a pale wall is invisible by construction.
- Hold the wider frame for the pop (event + both men's reactions — hands up, step back), don't cut to the horn. TED-Ed gives the popcorn its own clean vignette; ours gets the same courtesy inside the lab.

### S10 / S12 — kettle two-shots (was: both subjects lost in 9:16)
- **Never stage two people side-by-side across the width of a portrait frame.** In 9:16, two subjects fit only on a **diagonal**: separate them in depth (Δz ≥ 1.5) and height, not in x. Keep both heads inside the **central 60% column** (± ~0.9 of the camera axis).
- Working pattern for the egg beats: kettle low in the **foreground bottom-third**, the mocker's head and shoulders entering from the **top of frame** leaning down into it, hero mid-frame at the side. Vertical stacking uses the frame's long axis instead of fighting its short one.
- Camera farther back (z ≥ 6.5) with fov ≤ 30 — distance + mild compression keeps both in; stepping closer with a wider lens is what threw them out.
- Fallback the references both use: **shot / reverse-shot** (kettle action → victim's face) instead of a true two-shot. TED-Ed tells the egg beat exactly this way — the explosion is framed on the colleague's face, not on a wide of both men.

### S16 / S17 — the wave box (was: unclear, oversized)
- Clarity comes from **parts, not size**: door seam, handle/latch, rivets, and a window/mesh with the waves' glow inside. An unbroken slab reads as "big box"; a box with a door and a glowing window reads as "machine he built."
- Scale anchors in the same frame: the box sits **on the bench** (~1.2–1.4 units, hero's waist height on the bench) next to known-size objects (kettle, tools), and the hero's hand touches/pats it once. Anything oversized or undersized gets a **human ruler** in frame (TED-Ed's 6-ft oven rule).
- Order: one establishing wide (hero + bench + box) **before** any close-up, and silhouette the box against a darker background panel — never front-flat against a same-color wall.
- Borrow TED-Ed's cutaway for the "waves trapped" idea: one beat with the door open / panel cut so the waves are **seen bouncing inside** the box. Shown once, it explains the box for every later shot.

### S20 — the kitchens grid (was: abstract blocks)
- A repeated cell reads as "home" only if **every cell carries a domestic cue**: counter surface, cabinet doors, a window, warm light in some cells — and the hero microwave prop itself, window glowing, repeated at readable size.
- Depth cueing: **front row large and detailed** (one full kitchen, engineer standing in it — human anchor + scale), rows behind may simplify but must keep the same microwave silhouette + one warm window each. Identical dark blocks at identical value = pattern, not places.
- Warm, varied cell tones. A single dark-blue value across the grid is what made it read as abstract art.
- End the pull-back on recognition, not pattern: the last thing the eye lands on should be a normal countertop oven in a normal kitchen (TED-Ed's shrink ending lands the same way).

## Camera habits (all shots)
- Heights: mediums at character eye level (y ≈ 2.3–2.6). Low angle **only** for the giant oven (looking up = scale). High angle **only** for the grid reveal.
- Move or reframe every 1.5–2.5 s. Push = discovery; pull-back = reveal/scale. No static frames, no exceptions.
- The set carries the richness; characters stay simple, centered in the middle 60% column, and **anchored** — standing on floors, behind benches, hands on props. Nothing floats.

## Richness checklist — run on every shot before it ships
1. Three layers filled: foreground prop, midground action, dressed background — no empty layer.
2. The story object sits in the central column, physically anchored (in a pocket, on a bench, in a hand) — nothing floats.
3. Anything over- or under-sized has a human ruler beside it in the same frame.
4. Bright action plays against a dark backdrop; dark props against a bright one.
5. Every face shot keeps shoulders/torso in frame; head ≤ ~55% of frame height.
6. Two people = diagonal depth staging or shot/reverse-shot — never side-by-side width in 9:16.
7. Camera moves or reframes within 1.5–2.5 s; push for discovery, pull-back for scale.
8. At least one set element is visible that wasn't in the previous shot (screen, dials, tools, curtains, windows) — density accumulates, the set never repeats bare.
